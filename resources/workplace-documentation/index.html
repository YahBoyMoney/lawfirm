A usable workplace chronology tracks what happened, when, who knew, what was reported, and what changed afterward.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. It does not tell you whether to report, resign, or sign anything. It describes how to keep a record that can be reviewed if you decide to have it reviewed.

Keep a dated chronology

Contemporaneous notes are more persuasive than later recollection.

  • Log the date, the people present, what was said, and what happened next
  • Record it the same day when possible, and note when you are writing from memory
  • Keep the log somewhere personal, not on a work device or a work account
  • Note the business reason you were given for each decision, in the words used
  • Track schedule, duty, pay, and treatment changes that follow a report or a request

Put reports in a form that leaves a trace

Verbal reports are easy to dispute later.

  • Review any written reporting process in the handbook and keep a copy of the version you relied on
  • Confirm verbal conversations with a short, factual written follow-up
  • Keep copies of the complaint, the acknowledgment, and any investigation communication
  • Note who you reported to, their role, and the date
  • Save any response, including the absence of one, with its date

Collect the records you are entitled to keep

Focus on your own employment records, not company confidential material.

  • Offer letter, job description, handbook, and any policies you were asked to sign
  • Performance reviews, write-ups, awards, schedules, and time records
  • Pay stubs, wage statements, commission or bonus plans, and expense records
  • Leave, accommodation, or medical-related correspondence relating to you
  • Your personnel file, requested through the process your employer provides

If separation is coming or already happened

Access usually ends the same day the job does.

  • Save personal copies of your own records before the last day, without taking confidential company material
  • Keep the separation letter, final pay documents, and severance paperwork, note every signature deadline, and consider review before signing
  • Note every deadline printed on separation documents
  • Preserve messages, voicemails, and emails you already have on personal accounts
  • Write down the reason you were given and who gave it, on the day it happened

Preserve these before anything else

  • Your own dated log, kept on a personal device or account
  • Separation, severance, and final pay documents, including unsigned versions
  • Pay stubs, schedules, reviews, and any written discipline
  • Written reports you made and any response you received

Keep originals intact, work from copies, and do not send sensitive records through a public form.

Frequently asked

Common questions about this guide.

Can I copy my work emails before I leave?

Employer systems and policies control company material, and taking confidential company information can create separate problems. Focus on your own employment records, keep personal notes on personal accounts, and ask about the personnel file process.

Should I report internally first?

Whether and how to report is a decision with consequences that depend on your situation, and no web page can make it for you. What helps in every version is a dated record of what happened and what you were told.

How long should I keep documenting?

Through the end of the employment relationship and beyond, including how the employer responds afterward. The period after a report is often the most important part of the timeline.
